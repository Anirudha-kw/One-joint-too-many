A hand in space has six numbers to get right: three for position and three for orientation. The Franka Research 3 (FR3) has seven joints. The extra one means most hand poses can be reached by a whole family of arm configurations, which makes the arm flexible and its algorithms subtle. This post works through what that extra joint changes, from inverse kinematics to torque control, on a model of the real robot.

Every figure runs MuJoCo [1] in your browser through its official WebAssembly build, using the FR3 model from MuJoCo Menagerie [2]. The browser build and MuJoCo's Python bindings give bit-identical results on this model, and every number in the text comes from the same JavaScript the figures run (see Try it yourself).

The FR3

The FR3 is a 7-joint arm that is widely used in research. It has a torque sensor in every joint and an interface that accepts joint torques at 1 kHz [3], which makes it a natural platform for the torque controllers later in this post. Franka lists a 3 kg payload and 855 mm reach. The model used here is the arm alone, without the gripper, with Menagerie's fitted joint damping, friction and motor inertia.

Seven joints, six numbers

The chain alternates between joints that swing a link and joints that twist it. Joint 4 is the elbow, and joints 5–7 form the wrist. Two details matter for what follows. First, joints 1 and 3 share an axis whenever joint 2 is at zero, as in Menagerie's default "home" pose. At that pose the shoulder can only spin the upper arm about its own axis, so this post works from Franka's usual ready pose, \(q = (0, -\tfrac{\pi}{4}, 0, -\tfrac{3\pi}{4}, 0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{4})\), instead. Second, the elbow and wrist are offset by 8.25 cm and 8.8 cm, so the textbook spherical-wrist solution does not apply. The FR3's inverse kinematics has no simple closed form. Analytical solvers exist, but they fix one joint angle and solve for the rest [4].

The Jacobian \(J(q)\) maps the seven joint velocities to the hand's six-dimensional twist \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J\dot q\). With six rows and seven columns, \(J\) has at least a one-dimensional null space: joint velocities that move the arm without moving the hand. Following that direction traces out the self-motion, a curve of configurations that all put the hand in exactly the same pose.

For the ready pose, the self-motion runs 7.3 rad through joint space, measured as path length, before joint 6 hits its limit at either end. Along the way the shoulder swings through ±136° while the hand stays fixed to well under a micrometre. Not all of these configurations are equally good. Manipulability, \(\sqrt{\det J J^\top}\) [5], falls from 0.080 at the ready pose to 0.057 at the ends, and several joints end up near their limits. The redundancy is a resource, and the next section shows what happens if nothing manages it.

Following a path

To make the hand follow a moving target, differential IK turns a desired hand twist into joint velocities. The standard tool is damped least squares [6, 7]:

$$ \dot q = J^\top\!\left(J J^\top + \lambda^2 I\right)^{-1} \big(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_d + K e\big) \;+\; \big(I - J^{+} J\big)\, \dot q_0 , $$

where \(e\) is the six-dimensional pose error, \(\lambda\) keeps steps bounded near singularities, and \(J^{+}\) is the damped pseudo-inverse. The first term picks the smallest joint motion that moves the hand correctly. The second term is optional: it takes any desired joint motion \(\dot q_0\) and keeps only the part the hand cannot feel. A common choice is a pull toward a comfortable posture, \(\dot q_0 = k_0 (q_{\text{ready}} - q)\).

Without the second term, the null space is left to chance. The minimum-norm solution is not cyclic: when the hand traces a closed loop, the joints generally do not come back to where they started [8].

With a 2 s lap, the plain solver's configuration has already shifted 23° by the end of lap 2. In lap 5, joint 2 runs into its limit at 8.3 s. The hand then misses the circle by up to 90 mm, and the arm settles into a different, worse cycle 95° from the first. With the posture term, the error stays under 1.3 mm and the pose at the end of each lap changes by 0.1° across 20 laps. One line of code makes the difference.

What the motors feel

Torque control needs the dynamics:

$$ \big(M_{\text{links}}(q) + I_a\big)\,\ddot q + c(q,\dot q) + g(q) = \tau - \tau_{\text{friction}} - B\dot q . $$

The \(7\times7\) mass matrix now has strong off-diagonal coupling, and its diagonal has a second, constant part, \(I_a\). A geared motor's rotor spins \(N\) times faster than the joint, so its inertia appears at the joint multiplied by \(N^2\). At the ready pose the rotors add 11–28% to the diagonal entries of joints 1–4. For the wrist they dominate, accounting for 73%, 71% and 100% of the effective inertia of joints 5, 6 and 7. Joint 7's link is a thin disk spinning about its own axis, so almost everything the motor accelerates is its own rotor.

Gravity sets the static budget. At the ready pose the arm is folded and joint 4 carries the most load, 19 N·m or 22% of its limit. Stretched out horizontally, joint 2 needs 51 N·m (58%), and a payload pushes it past its 87 N·m limit at about 4.75 kg. That is comfortably above the rated 3 kg, and the margin is what remains for accelerating the arm.

Torque control

With torques available, there are three common ways to move the hand. All three add the model's gravity and Coriolis terms, \(c + g\), to the torque they command.

  • Joint impedance: run the IK above to get a joint trajectory, then make each joint a spring-damper, \(\tau = K_q(q_d - q) - D_q\dot q + c + g\).
  • Cartesian impedance [9]: put the spring at the hand. A pose error \(e\) produces a wrench, and \(J^\top\) maps it to joint torques: \(\tau = J^\top\!\big(K e - D\dot{\mathbf{x}}\big) + c + g + \tau_{\text{null}}\).
  • Operational-space control [10]: also cancel the arm's inertia as seen at the hand, \(\Lambda = (J M^{-1} J^\top)^{-1}\), so every direction responds the same way: \(\tau = J^\top \Lambda\big(\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_d + \omega^2 e + 2\omega\dot e\big) + c + g + \tau_{\text{null}}\).

The null-space torque \(\tau_{\text{null}}\) plays the role of the posture term in IK. It is a joint-space spring toward the ready pose, projected so that it does not disturb the hand. For operational-space control the projection is weighted by \(M\), the "dynamically consistent" version [10].

The impedance controllers lag the moving target because they only react to error. Operational-space control feeds forward the target's acceleration through \(\Lambda\), so it tracks an order of magnitude better and is also stiffer against the push. Stiffness is not always the goal, though. A Cartesian impedance controller's hand behaves like a spring you chose, which is exactly what you want for contact and for working around people. The unmodeled payload hurts every controller, because each one relies on the model's \(g(q)\).

The elbow push isolates the null space. With the hand held still, a 20 N push on the elbow for 0.5 s under operational-space control moves the elbow 106 mm when there is no null-space term, and it stays there. Nothing pulls it back, and joint friction holds it. With the posture term the elbow moves only 9 mm and returns to within 4 mm, while the hand moves 1 mm. The residual offset is friction again. About 1 N·m of friction per joint can resist a soft posture spring, and the residual shrank from 16 mm to 2 mm as the null-space stiffness went from 10 to 60 N·m/rad.

The pose matters too. Holding the circle's start point instead, where joint 2 is at −18°, the same push moves the elbow about 1 mm with or without the null-space term. With joint 2 that close to zero, joints 1 and 3 nearly line up again, and the self-motion becomes mostly the two of them counter-rotating, which hardly moves the elbow. That is why Hold in Figure 5 returns to the ready pose.

Stiff actuators and integrators

Menagerie ships the FR3 with position actuators: a stiff PD loop at each joint, with gains \(k_p\) up to 4500 N·m/rad and \(k_v\) up to 450 N·m·s/rad, and forces clipped to the torque limits. It also sets MuJoCo's implicitfast integrator. The reason is that MuJoCo's default, semi-implicit Euler, treats joint damping implicitly but the actuators' velocity gain explicitly. A large \(k_v\) acting on a small inertia, such as the wrist's roughly 0.1 kg·m², then needs a very small timestep. implicitfast includes the actuators' velocity derivatives in the implicit step.

Nothing explodes, which is the subtle part. With Euler at Menagerie's 2 ms timestep, the explicit damping overshoots on every step, but the force limits clip it. The result is a bounded chatter: every joint's velocity flips sign on every step, and the actuators sit at their force limits 75–100% of the time. The arm never settles within 0.01 rad of its targets, and the error grows with the timestep. At 1 ms, Euler settles, though joint 7 still buzzes against its limit. implicitfast settles in 0.38 s at every timestep up to 8 ms. Its remaining 5.4 mrad error is gravity: a position actuator without gravity compensation stops where \(k_p e = g(q)\), and joint 4 carries 19 N·m against a \(k_p\) of 3500 N·m/rad.

The core of operational-space control in fr3core.js:

const Lam = invSPD(sandwich(J, 6, N, invSPD(M, N)), 6);   // Λ = (J M⁻¹ Jᵀ)⁻¹
const F = mulMV(Lam, 6, 6, acc);                           // Λ (ẍ_d + ω² e + 2ω ė)
const tau = mulMtV(J, 6, N, F);                            // Jᵀ F, then + null space + c + g

What the model leaves out

This is a simulation of a published model, not the robot. The physics models here drop all geometry, so there are no contacts or self-collisions. Every FR3 link has explicit inertial parameters, so the dynamics are unchanged, and the build script checks this. Friction is MuJoCo's simple joint frictionloss. There is no motor electrical dynamics, sensor noise, communication delay or joint flexibility, and the real FR3's interface enforces rate and safety limits that these controllers ignore. Treat the numbers as showing how the methods compare, not as predictions for hardware.
